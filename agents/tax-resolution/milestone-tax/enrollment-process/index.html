Tax resolution with Milestone Tax Solutions

Funding Tier now sells tax resolution for clients who owe the IRS or a state tax agency. The backend is Milestone Tax Solutions, LLC: they represent the client, pull transcripts, negotiate with the IRS and service the file. Funding Tier runs the sales call, enrolls the client in Milestone's CRM and hands them to Milestone's welcome call team. It is sold in two separate sales on the same client.

Phase 1 · first call

Investigation & representation

A consultative intake call. You uncover the tax problem, run the financial interview, then enroll the client for a flat investigation fee.

  • Fee: $500 standard (federal). $995 when the case is urgent or covers federal and state.
  • Eligible: owes at least $10,000 federal, or $20,000 on a state-only case. No excluded states.
  • Documents: Client Service Agreement + Payment Authorization (sent by you). IRS authorization forms and the tax-information consent come from Milestone's welcome call team.
  • Milestone then spends about 2.5 weeks from the payment clearing (4–5 for a state case) pulling transcripts and building the financial profile.
Phase 2 · second call

Resolution

Once the investigation is done, a Phase 2 opportunity appears in the CRM with Milestone's findings, the price and the script to use. You pitch the resolution plan and collect payment.

  • Fee: flat, priced by IRS balance ($1,495 to $26,495; custom above $300K). Pay-in-full discount of 10–20%.
  • Payment: pay in full (10–15% of clients), a loan (35–40%), or a down payment plus up to 12 monthly payments (about 50%).
  • About 70% of funded Phase 1 clients go on to fund Phase 2 (Milestone's average).
  • Documents: blanket Client Service Agreement + Payment Authorization (you), Form 2848 power of attorney (welcome call team).

Two hats: intake specialist, then performer

Milestone's top closers describe the job as two different roles. On Phase 1 you are an intake specialist: your only job is to understand the client's situation deeply enough to know why they are on the phone today. On Phase 2 you are a performer: you deliver Milestone's resolution script using the pain the client told you in Phase 1. A well-run Phase 1 makes Phase 2 close itself; a Phase 1 sold on promises ("we'll get your debt to zero") produces Phase 1s that never close a Phase 2.

Phase 1 is sold on protection and representation. Phase 2 is sold on peace of mind, not on savings. Do not build a savings calculator pitch around tax — the IRS numbers are not known until the investigation is done.

Who we are looking for

Milestone does not take every case, and the script says so. The best cases are clients with a real, pressing reason to fix it now (a letter, a garnishment, a license, a job, a home purchase, a passport) and enough income to pay for help.

  • Strong fits: small trade-business owners (plumbing, HVAC, contractors) who are good at the work and behind on the books; truck drivers and other 1099 contractors, often hit with a Substitute for Return; clients with multiple unfiled years.
  • Poor fits: clients with high disposable income and a small balance who only want the debt "taken to zero" (they will not get a reduction), and clients with no income and no reason to act.
  • Federal first. Most cases are federal. A state-only case should owe at least $20,000 to the state; states are slower and offer fewer options.
  • Seasonality: demand peaks mid-February to April 15, stays strong through June (people who filed and can't pay), dips July–August, and bumps before the October 15 extension deadline.

How Funding Tier gets paid (from the signed agreement)

  • Phase 1: 50% of the cleared investigation fee.
  • Phase 2: 40% / 50% / 60% of what the client has actually paid. The tier is set by how many Phase 2 deals funded the prior month (1–24 / 25–49 / 50+). A deal counts as funded when the full payment or first installment clears, and its rate locks at that moment.
  • Installments pay Funding Tier as each one clears. If a client stops paying, charges back or cancels, that commission is clawed back.
  • Nothing pays until Milestone's recorded welcome call is done — so a clean handoff is part of getting paid.
  • Refund window: 15 days from signing; 3 business days in California, Texas and Indiana. A refund inside the window claws back the commission.
  • Paid weekly by ACH, about two weeks after the client's payment clears; Milestone can hold 10% for 180 days.

Discovery

Open the call and find the problem

Aim to learn why they are calling today within the first five minutes.

Agent · inbound“Thank you for calling the Tax Resolution Services Department, this is ____. This call is recorded for quality and compliance. How can I help you today?”
Agent · outbound (required at the start of every outbound call)“Hi ____, this is ____ with the Tax Resolution Services Department. I'm calling on behalf of Milestone Tax Solutions, LLC about tax-resolution services — helping people who owe the IRS or the state get represented and resolve their tax accounts. This call is recorded for quality and compliance. Do you have a few minutes?”
Agent · verbal opt-in (before giving any program details)“Based on your situation, we have a tax-resolution program from Milestone Tax Solutions that may help resolve your tax accounts, but I need a verbal opt-in to provide more details. Do you opt in to hearing about Milestone Tax Solutions and its services, and to being contacted directly by us or our representatives?”
Agent“Great. Tell me about your tax issue.”
Why the opener has three parts The Milestone agreement requires the recorded verbal opt-in on every call (Exhibit C) and, on outbound calls, naming Milestone Tax Solutions as the seller and saying the call is about tax-resolution services right at the start (16 C.F.R. §310.4(d)). The recording notice covers all-party-consent states, including California. Never say or imply you're with the IRS, the Treasury or a state tax agency — if a caller asks, "No — we're a private company, on your side of the table."
  1. What tax years are you dealing with?
  2. Do you have any unfiled tax returns? What years are unfiled?
  3. How much do you currently owe — or how much do you think you owe?
  4. Is this a federal tax debt, state, or both?
  5. Is your tax debt personal or business related?
  6. Is the tax debt from 1099 or W-2 income?
  7. Are you currently employed? Are you 1099 or W-2?
  8. Have you received any letters from the IRS yet?
    • Did the letter come standard mail or certified?
    • There's a notice number in the upper right-hand corner — can you read that out, and the due date? (Ask them to photograph or scan it and email it to you.)
  9. Are any of your wages being garnished yet?
    • Would a wage garnishment jeopardize your job or put you in a hard financial situation?
  10. Have any of your bank accounts been seized yet?
  11. What are your expectations or goals to resolve your tax debt? Why now? Any concerns?
Dig to the second and third level "I owe $15,000" is not an answer. Ask how they know, where the number came from, what happens if it doesn't get handled, and why now. Real clients with a real problem will answer; paid actors and tire-kickers self-select out when the questions get specific. If they mention something big in passing (a death in the family, a job, a license), stop and ask about it.
Use the word "yet" — and nothing more "Have they garnished your paycheck yet?" makes the client realize enforcement is coming without you having to threaten anything. Do not pile on intimidation.
Urgent flags — note these now If the client says a paycheck garnishment is about to start, a bank account is about to be levied, they have an audit coming, or a revenue officer is assigned, the case is urgent. Tick the urgent box in the CRM (Phase 1 becomes $995) and tell the welcome call team so they get the Form 2848 on file within days instead of weeks. Ask for the revenue officer's business card if they have one.

3
Financial interview

Build the budget the way the IRS will

The IRS judges ability to pay using its own allowable-expense standards, not the client's real spending. Collect the categories the IRS counts.

  1. Marital status — how do you file? Single, married filing jointly or separately? Married filing jointly means a co-client and two signatures on the documents.
  2. How many are in your household? Any dependents?
  3. How much do you make per month (gross)? W-2 or 1099? What do you do for work? How often are you paid? The CRM asks pay frequency and the amount per paycheck.
  4. Do you rent or own? How much per month? Any equity in your home? A home equity line? Any other properties or rental income?
  5. On average, how much do you pay for utilities per month? Power, gas, water, sewer, electric only. Cable, internet and cell phone do not count.
  6. Do you have a car payment? How much? Car insurance? Gas?
  7. Any childcare or child support, going in or coming out?
  8. Monthly medical expenses — co-pays, prescriptions?
  9. Do you have any credit cards? Available credit? How much? Only log unsecured loans if they come up.
  10. How much do you spend on groceries per month? Goes in the CRM's "food" field.
Agent“If the IRS demanded a monthly payment from you, how much could you afford to pay? Even if it were tight?”
Agent“Our protection extends to your assets as well. We need to be able to identify any assets that may be vulnerable. Do you have any other assets we should be mindful of, that may need protection?” (e.g. 401(k), stocks, rental property)

The pivot

Brief hold, then present what the IRS will see

Agent“I'm going to put you on a brief hold and review your case. When I come back, we'll discuss the details. Assuming everything looks good on our end, we will go over our process and discuss pricing.” (Mute for at least a minute.)

When you come back, the call forks on the client's disposable income (net income minus expenses, from the CRM calculation):

More than $400 a month left over
Agent“Let me explain what I am seeing. Your net income is $4,500, your expenses are $3,500. If you were to contact the IRS and try to deal with this, they are going to ask you similar questions and see similar numbers. You owe the IRS $35,000. After they analyze your expenses and realize you have about $1,000 of disposable income, what do you think they are going to tell you?”

Pause. Let them speak first. They will say "they'll want all of it." Then: “We wouldn't want our client to have that conversation with the IRS, agreed? The good news is we can take this case on and represent you. Let me explain our process.”

Less than $400, or negative
Agent“It's obvious that you need help. After expenses, you have approximately $100 of disposable income. I have to ask — how are you surviving?”

Pause and let them describe the hardship again. This is where you learn what discovery missed: maxed cards, family helping, another income. Then: “You certainly need someone to make this argument to the IRS on your behalf. Let me explain how our process works.”

If the client pushes back on the number "I don't have that much — I've got kids." Agree that those things matter to them, then return to the point: "If the IRS sees that you have that money left over, what do you think they're going to say?"

The pitch

Explain the two-phase process

Never say "it's called" — just describe what happens.

Agent“We work in a two-phase process. The first phase is investigation and representation. We contact the IRS on your behalf and file all the necessary documents to take over all communications. At this point we will identify and address any collection activity you may need protection against — wage garnishments, account levies and property seizures. Once under our representation, we will conduct a full investigation into your case:
1. We will order the transcripts from the IRS and identify exactly how much you owe and how many penalties and interest were applied.
2. We will conduct a full compliance check, looking for any red flags — missing returns, audits and other IRS corrections.
We are also going to build a full financial profile that accurately represents your current financial situation.

We'll take all of that and use it in the second phase of our process, which is the resolution. Once we know the full extent of the tax issue and everything you are facing, we will be able to devise a full resolution plan for your case. There are many ways to resolve a tax issue — penalty abatement programs that address the penalties and interest you absorbed, non-collectible status programs, installment agreement negotiations, and offer in compromise, which is an IRS settlement process. A tax company cannot responsibly promise a resolution upfront; identifying the right solution requires a proper investigation of your tax issues.”
Lean on "the IRS knows, not us" "You say you owe $20,000 and you got the letter. I'd love to take your word for it, but what matters is what the IRS says. We're going to find that out, then build a plan to solve it." This works on the client who is sure of the number and on the client who has no idea.

Read the required disclosures before you send documents

Every enrollment, every client (Agreement, Exhibit B). Milestone's welcome call will have the client re-affirm each one — if they sound surprised, the file stalls.

  1. Results depend on your circumstances and on what the IRS and state decide. No particular result is promised or guaranteed.
  2. Fees are for the professional services performed. They are not a percentage of, or contingent on, how much tax, penalty or interest is reduced.
  3. Enrolling doesn't by itself stop IRS or state collection activity. Milestone acts for you once authorized but can't guarantee all collection activity stops.
  4. Whether you qualify for an Offer in Compromise, Currently Not Collectible status, penalty abatement or an installment agreement is decided by the taxing authority based on your finances.
  5. I work for an independent marketing company, not for Milestone, and we're paid by Milestone for referring your enrollment.
  6. Milestone is a tax-resolution firm. It's not a law firm, doesn't give legal advice or representation, and no attorney-client relationship is created.
  7. You can cancel for a full refund within 15 days of signing — or within 3 business days if you live in California, Texas or Indiana. After that the fee is earned and isn't refundable.
  8. If you later add another entity or more tax years, the fee goes up, and any increase is quoted and agreed in writing before that work starts.

Final notice of intent to levy & right to a hearing. Levy can follow 30 days later.

  • Always get the letter: notice number (top right), date and due date. A photo or scan by email is fine.
  • Bank levy: takes only what is in the account that day, then the bank holds it for 21 days before sending it to the IRS — the best window to get it released. The IRS can levy again.
  • Wage garnishment: most people lose 50–80% of a paycheck. Ask when payroll is processed (often Wednesday for a Friday payday). If it's too late to stop this one, the pitch is stopping the next one: "the sooner we start, the sooner that money stays in your pocket."

  • Revenue officer vs ACS: most cases sit with the Automated Collection System (call centers). Higher balances (around $50K for business, $200K+ personal) get a revenue officer who can visit, levy and seize. A revenue officer is an urgent case.
  • Lien vs levy: a lien is a claim on assets; a levy is taking them.
  • Married filing jointly: both spouses are liable for joint federal debt.

The collection statute runs from when the IRS assesses the debt, not the tax year. A 2014 return filed in 2019 is assessed in 2019, so an "old" letter may still be very collectible. Pulling transcripts is a passive request and does not reset any clock. Don't raise the statute on calls — it only teaches clients to wait. How the IRS decides what a client can pay

The IRS takes income, subtracts its allowable expenses and calls the rest monthly disposable income. That number decides whether a client fits an installment agreement, an offer in compromise or hardship (CNC).

  • National standards (by household size): food, clothing, housekeeping, personal care, miscellaneous.
  • Local standards (by county): housing, utilities, vehicle ownership and operating costs, public transit. The client can claim the standard even if they spend less.
  • Actual expenses, if documented: health insurance, medical, child or dependent care, court-ordered payments, minimum student loan payments, necessary business expenses.
  • Luxury or excessive expenses don't count. Standards change every year. The closer a client is to IRS hardship, the more options they usually qualify for — even with a good income in a high-cost area.

Federal vs state

Milestone handles federal, state, or both; most clients are federal only. States require their own power of attorney, accept it at their own pace, and offer different options (some only installment agreements). A client can owe a state they don't live in — e.g. gambling winnings in Oklahoma for a Texas resident. Winnings in a no-income-tax state like Nevada are reported to the IRS instead.
